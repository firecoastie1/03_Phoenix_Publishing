Chapter 19
The Border Dispute

VICTOR

The bond pulled him before he even opened his eyes.

It wasn't a sound, or a word, or an image. It was warmth, a steady, low hum, like a second heartbeat in his chest. Amelia was awake. He didn't know how he knew, but he knew, and it made something loosen in his ribs that had been knotted tight for weeks.

He got up. Washed his face. Put on his coat. Didn't think about going to her cottage. Didn't need to. The bond hummed, steady and open, and that was enough.

He walked into the compound's common room at eight AM and David was already there, coffee in hand, eyes narrow.

"You look different," David said.

Victor sat at the table. "How?"

"I don't know. You look — I don't know. Less tired." David studied him. "Is it the bond?"

"David."

"I'm just asking. You've been —" He gestured vaguely. "Running yourself into the ground since your father died. But you look different. Better. Or worse. I can't tell."

Victor didn't answer. He couldn't tell either. He felt steady. That was the word. Steady. For the first time in three years, he felt steady. And it terrified him.

The war room filled quickly. Perseus arrived with maps. Emila came with supply reports. Josiah stood by the window, arms crossed, quiet as always.

Then Marco walked in with news.

"Alpha," he said, and his voice was tight. "Border runners from the south. They've been pushed back three miles in the last week. Hanley's men are moving fast."

The room shifted. Shoulders straightened. Voices dropped. Everyone knew what that meant.

Gage Hanley. Alpha of the rival wolf pack, three hundred miles south in Louisiana swampland. Broad, scarred, aggressive. Believed wolves were superior to all other shifter types. Believed the Blackwood Pack's willingness to work with other shifter types, to bond with them, was degeneracy.

And now he was testing the northern boundary. Pushing. Looking for weakness.

Perseus spread the map on the table and pointed at the southern border. "They're not attacking. Not yet. They're probing. Testing our response time. How fast we mobilize. Whether we fight back or hold the line."

"David's men are there?" Victor said.

"Yes," David said. "But they're light on numbers. If Hanley pushes harder, we'll need a senior warrior to take command."

"I'll go," Victor said.

No one answered immediately. Perseus looked at him.

"You're the Alpha," he said quietly. "You hold the center. I'll handle the edges."

"You don't have to handle —"

"Victor." Perseus's voice was firm but not unkind. "You hold the center. I handle the edges. That's what you taught me. That's what this pack needs from you right now."

Victor sat back in his chair and nodded. It was the right call. It was the responsible call. But it didn't stop something cold from opening in his chest, because the edges felt important now. The bond had made him hyper-aware of everything outside his immediate circle. He felt the pack's tension like pressure in his skull. He felt David's need to act, Emila's worry, Josiah's quiet calculation. He felt Perseus's certainty.

And he felt Amelia. Somewhere down the road, she was waking up, and the bond hummed like a second heartbeat in his chest, and he wondered if she could feel his attention, the way his gaze kept returning to the southern border like it was a threat he couldn't name.

Perseus rolled up the map and stood. "I leave by noon."

David nodded. Emila packed supplies. Josiah disappeared without a word.

But Perseus didn't head for the door. He walked over to Victor and leaned in close, his voice low enough that no one else could hear.

"You're distracted," he said.

Victor looked up at him.

"The bond," Perseus said quietly. "Is it affecting your judgment?"

Victor didn't answer. He couldn't. Not because he didn't have an answer, but because he didn't have one at all. He felt distracted. He felt — he felt everything. The bond had opened something in him, made him aware of things he'd spent years burying under duty and control and the weight of being Alpha. And now he couldn't close them back up.

Perseus watched him for a long moment. Then he nodded once and turned away.

The nod wasn't angry. It wasn't accusatory. But it was doubt. And it was the kind of doubt that grew in the quiet, in the moments between words, in the spaces where a man was supposed to be certain and instead was just uncertain.

When the war room emptied, Victor stood alone at the table, staring at the map and trying to figure out what Perseus meant.

AMELIA

Amelia's cottage was quiet in the morning. This was not the heavy, oppressive quiet that had settled over her for weeks — the quiet of an empty house, an empty bed, an empty life. This was different. This was the quiet of a woman who had slept through the night for the first time in months and woken up without that familiar ache in her chest.

She made coffee. She sat at her kitchen table with her research notes. She looked at the shelf where she'd placed her grandmother's packet, the one that smelled like fresh pancakes and bacon and her grandmother's kitchen. She had put it there the night before, after the kitchen scene, after he'd offered her a room and looked at her mouth and not looked away.

Luna sat on her lap, purring, tail flicking in slow, contented circles.

"You like it here," Amelia said. Luna didn't answer. She was a cat. Cats didn't answer questions that required yes or no. But she purred louder, which was close enough.

Amelia picked up one of her research notes and tried to read it. She had been studying historical records of mate bonds: patterns, timing, correlations with Hollow King activity. She'd found something troubling: every time a mate bond was formed, the Hollow King struck harder within weeks. It was a pattern, but it wasn't a correlation she understood. Was the Hollow King hunting bonds? Or was he just drawn to them, like moths to flame?

She didn't have an answer. But she suspected the answer was bad.

She set the notes down and stared at the wall.

The bond hummed in her chest, low and steady. She could feel him, not his thoughts, not his emotions, just his presence. A warmth, a steadiness, a hum that felt like a second heartbeat. It was comforting. It was also confusing. Because it made her feel less alone, and less alone was exactly what she had been trying to manage. She didn't want to get used to this. She didn't want to get used to having someone who hummed in her chest like a second heartbeat.

But she did. That was the problem. She did want it.

VICTOR

Perseus left at noon. Victor walked him to the gate and watched him ride south, his cloak flapping in the wind, his horse moving fast across the flat ground. The bond hummed — Amelia's presence, steady and distant. He couldn't tell what she was doing. He could only feel the shape of her attention, like a pressure in his chest, warm and quiet.

He turned away and walked back to the war room.

David was still there, pacing, jaw tight, hands clenched.

"He's going to walk right into it," David said.

"Perseus?" Victor said.

"He's walking right into a negotiation with Gage Hanley. A man who thinks wolves are better than everyone else and has been looking for an excuse to test us since the day you took over. Perseus is one man. Hanley has a pack behind him."

"He's Perseus," Victor said.

"I know who he is. I'm not saying he can't handle it. I'm saying —" David stopped, ran a hand through his hair. "I'm saying you should have gone. I know you said you'd handle the center, but this is an edge. This is territory. This is—"

"David."

"You're different, Victor. I don't know if it's the bond or something else, but you're — it's like you're listening to something we're not hearing."

Victor looked at him. "What do you hear?"

"Nothing," David said. "That's the point. I hear nothing. And I'm supposed to stand here and wait while Perseus rides south to talk to a man who doesn't want to talk."

Victor didn't answer. He couldn't. Because David was right. The bond had changed the way he experienced the world. He felt things more deeply. He was aware of things he'd been blind to before. And he wasn't sure if that was strength or weakness.

He looked at the map on the table and thought of the southern border. The Hanley pack. Gage Hanley, the man who thought the Blackwood Pack was weak.

*Perseus was the right choice for the negotiation.* Victor knew it. But knowing it didn't stop the cold, tight feeling in his chest, and he wondered if it ever would.

He walked to the window and looked south.

The horizon was flat and empty. He couldn't see Perseus. He couldn't see the southern border. He could only feel the hum in his chest: Amelia's presence, steady and warm, and the faint, distant pulse of something else. A pull. A tension. Something that felt like a pack moving fast and hard, like a hundred wolves running toward a fight they didn't need to have.

The bond hummed. He closed his eyes. He tried to steady himself.

But the hum didn't stop. It didn't even fade. It stayed steady and warm, like a second heartbeat in his chest, and he wondered if this was what it felt like to be alive.
