# Chapter 5: The Patrol

The morning started like any other — with the smell of coffee and the sound of boots on the porch, followed by a cough in the doorway.

Amelia's clinic smelled like the same things it always did — antiseptic, dried herbs, the faint scent of liniment from the jars lined along the shelves. Normal smells. Predictable smells. The kind of smells that made the world feel safe.

She made herself a cup of coffee, opened the front door, and went to work.

But her hands were tingling.

She told herself it was nothing. It was the coffee. It was the damp. It was nerves from yesterday.

The tingling didn't stop.

---

The first patient was Mrs. Calloway, who'd dropped off soup last time and needed a new prescription for her blood pressure. Amelia wrote it out, handed it to her with a smile, and watched her leave through the window. The second was a young woman with a sprained wrist from slipping on the ice. The third was a farmer with a cut on his hand from a mower blade.

She treated all of them. She did everything right. She knew what she was doing.

But her hands were tingling. She told herself it would fade. It always faded. It had faded the night before, hadn't it?

It faded again by afternoon. But it didn't go away completely. It lingered, a low hum in the back of her palms, like electricity after a storm — not enough to feel, but enough to know it was there.

She told herself it was fine.

It wasn't fine.

---

Victor

Victor rode back to the compound after dark.

His horse was tired. So was he. He'd spent the last four hours riding the northern boundary, checking the markers, looking for signs, trying to find whatever it was that had moved his heart and his hands and his mind in a direction he couldn't control. He hadn't found anything. No tracks. No scent. No disturbance. Just the feeling, steady and persistent, like a compass needle that wouldn't settle.

He was exhausted. He was shaken. He was a man who had been raised to believe that he understood the world, that he knew how things worked, that he could predict the behavior of wolves and weather and men, and none of that mattered anymore because something had shifted in him and he didn't know how to fix it.

He reined his horse at the compound gate. The stone walls rose up around him, dark and familiar. The smell of woodsmoke and cooking food drifted from the great hall. He could hear voices — pack members, probably, sitting around tables, talking, laughing, living their lives like everything was normal.

It wasn't normal. Nothing was normal. But he was the Alpha, and Alphas didn't let the pack see them shaken.

He dismounted, tied his horse, and walked inside.

The great hall was warm and full of light. Fires crackled in the stone hearths. Pack members sat at long wooden tables, eating, talking, laughing. David was at the far end of the room, arm draped over the back of his chair, talking loud enough for half the room to hear. Perseus sat near the door, his expression unreadable, his eyes tracking every person who entered and left. He looked up the moment Victor walked in.

And he noticed.

Perseus had been trained to notice things. It was his job — to watch the pack, to read the room, to understand what people were saying and what they weren't. He noticed that Victor's hands were clenched at his sides. He noticed that his knuckles were white. He noticed that his jaw was tight, his shoulders tense, his eyes with which are usually sharp, focused, and commanding were distant and unfocused, like he was looking at something no one else could see.

Victor caught him looking. Perseus looked away.

"Alpha," Perseus said, his voice quiet. "You look like hell."

Victor didn't smile. He didn't frown. He just kept walking, past the tables, past the fires, past the pack, toward the head of the table where he was supposed to sit and eat and be normal and pretend that nothing was wrong.

"I'm fine," he said.

Perseus watched him go. He didn't believe him. But he didn't press. Not yet.

David noticed.

"Did the Alpha get spooked by a squirrel?" he said, loud enough for the whole room to hear. He grinned. "Because I can help you with that. I've got a trap set up. We can go out tomorrow. I'll catch it. You can put it down."

Laughter rippled through the room. David was joking, mostly.

Victor didn't laugh. He sat at the head of the table. He stared at the fire. He thought about the pull, the tingling, the feeling that something had shifted in the world and he couldn't explain it to anyone. He thought about the way his hands had been shaking on his horse, the way his heart had hammered against his ribs, the way the feeling had stayed with him all day, like a shadow he couldn't outrun.

He didn't tell anyone. He never told anyone.

David's smile faded. He looked at Perseus. Perseus looked back. They both knew something was wrong. They just didn't know what.

Amelia

That afternoon, Amelia sat at her kitchen table and touched the moon tattoo on her wrist.

It was something she'd never done before. She'd looked at it — of course she had, she'd looked at it a thousand times, admired the ink, remembered the night her grandmother had held her hand steady while the needle moved across her skin — but she'd never touched it. Not like this. Not with the deliberate intention of feeling it.

But her hand had moved without asking her. Her fingers had found the edge of her sleeve. Her thumb had traced the curve of the crescent moon, the little stars scattered around it, the faint line of her grandmother's handwriting that had been pressed into the design — *"Read the sky, and the sky will read you back."*

She'd never touched it before.

Today, her fingers burned.

It wasn't a lot — not a real burn, not a pain. But it was there, a sharp, sudden heat that crawled across her skin like the mark was alive, like it was responding to something she couldn't feel but that was definitely there.

She pulled her hand back. She stared at the tattoo. She touched it again.

Nothing.

She touched it a third time. Nothing.

She told herself it was nothing. It was her imagination. It was the coffee. It was nerves.

It didn't stop feeling warm for hours.

---

That night, Amelia dreamed of a wolf.

Not a real wolf — not the kind she'd seen in stories or in pictures, small and furry and cute. This wolf was larger than anything that should have been possible. It was the size of a horse. Its fur was dark, almost black, but not quite — like storm clouds, like deep water, like the space between stars. Its eyes were amber — bright, sharp, intelligent — and they were looking right at her.

She didn't know where she was. She didn't know what she was doing in the dream. But she knew, with a certainty that was deeper than reason, that the wolf was not a threat. It was watching her. Not with hunger, not with fear, but with recognition.

The wolf took a step toward her.

She woke.

Her hands were tingling. Her chest was aching. Her heart was racing. And the dream was still there — the wolf, the amber eyes, the feeling of recognition — burning behind her eyelids like a photograph left too long in the sun.

She lay on her back. She stared at the ceiling. And she wondered why, for the first time in her life, she didn't want to be alone.
