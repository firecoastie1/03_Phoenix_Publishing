# Chapter 9: The Return

Victor

The mist thickened. The forest went quiet. And neither of them knew what to do next.

Then he blinked. He shook his head. He didn't know why, but the feeling between them was still there — the pull, the tension, the invisible thread stretching from her chest to his. He couldn't let her walk back through the forest alone. He couldn't explain why he knew that. He just did.

"Amelia," he said. He didn't know why he said her name again. It felt right in his mouth. "You can't stay out here."

She looked at him. She didn't answer right away. She was looking at him like she was trying to figure him out the same way he was trying to figure her out.

"Why not?" she asked.

"Because you're a human woman in a forest that's not safe." He didn't know why he was saying that. It wasn't entirely true. The forest wasn't safe for her. It was also not safe for anyone else. But he didn't know how to say that. He didn't know how to say any of it.

She was quiet. Then she said, "I live near here."

"I heard you."

"Then you know I can take care of myself."

He wanted to tell her she was right, that she was the strongest person he'd ever met in his life. But, he didn't.

"I know you can," he said instead. "But I'm not asking you to go back alone."

She studied him. He studied her back. The bond between them hummed — low, steady, like a heartbeat. She didn't say anything for a long moment. Then she said, "What are you saying?"

"I'm saying you're coming with me."

"To where?"

"To the compound. It's safe. There are — there are people there. Guards. You'll be fine."

She thought about it. He could see her thinking — the way her eyes narrowed slightly, the way her mouth pressed into a thin line, the way her hands flexed at her sides. She was weighing him. Deciding whether to trust him. The weight of that decision sat on his chest like a stone.

"Okay," she said.

He didn't expect that. "Okay?"

"Yes. I'm coming with you. But I'm not — I'm not your prisoner. I'm not doing anything you're making me do. I'm deciding this. Do you understand?"

He nodded. He understood perfectly. "Yes. You're deciding this."

"Good."

He turned. He walked toward his horse. He didn't look back. He didn't need to. He could feel her behind him — the bond pulling him, the thread stretching between them, warm and bright and undeniable.

He reached the horse, and untied it from the tree, then turned to her.

"Get on," he said.

He held out his hand. His palm was sweating. He hated that his palm was sweating.

She looked at his hand. She looked at him. She looked at the horse. And then, without a word, she took his hand.

His fingers closed around hers.

The bond flared — sharp, bright, immediate — and for a second he saw stars behind his eyes. He gritted his teeth. She gasped. Then she pulled her hand back like she'd been burned.

"Sorry," he said. "I forget, human strength. I mean, I wasn't —"

He stopped. He didn't know what he was going to say. He didn't know how to explain a sensation he couldn't name to a woman who didn't know he could name it.

"It's fine," she said. "I'm fine. I can —" She gestured at the horse. "I can get up. I'm not made of glass."

He was quiet. He wanted to tell her she wasn't. He wanted to tell her she was something else entirely. He didn't.

"Let me help," he said instead.

He stepped closer. He reached for her waist — then stopped. His hand hovered. He didn't know if he should. He didn't know if she would let him.

She looked at his hand. She looked at his face. And she nodded.

His fingers closed around her waist. She was smaller than he expected. Smaller than he wanted. His hand nearly reached all the way across her. He felt the heat of her through her clothes, the softness of her, the way her breath caught when he pulled her up.

She was standing in front of him now, her hands on his shoulders for balance, her eyes wide and startled and trusting in a way that made his chest ache. He let go of her waist. She stepped back. The bond dimmed.

He swung his leg over the saddle and mounted. Then he reached down and steadied her as she climbed up behind him. Her back pressed to his chest. Her hands gripped his jacket. For one second — just one second — they were pressed together, and the bond went so bright he thought he might actually see it.

---

Amelia

The ride back was the longest three miles of her life.

She sat behind Victor on the horse, her hands gripping his jacket, her body pressed close to his back, and every inch of her skin was alive. The bond was there — not as strong as it had been when they first met, not the detonation she'd felt at the stream, but there, a low hum in the back of her skull like a phone on silent, like a voice she couldn't hear but could feel. She tried to ignore it. She couldn't.

She tried to think about anything else. She tried to think about her clinic. She tried to think about the herbs she needed to gather. She tried to think about Luna waiting at home. But she couldn't stop thinking about the man in front of her — his shoulders, solid and warm under her hands, the way his muscles moved when the horse walked, the way his breathing changed when she shifted behind him.

She didn't know what this was. She didn't know what he was. She didn't know anything.

But she knew one thing: she didn't want to be away from him.

And that scared her more than the forest had.

---

They reached the compound as the sun was setting.

Amelia saw it from a distance — not a building, exactly, but a complex. Multiple structures connected by wooden walkways, surrounded by high fences and trees. It looked like something from a movie, not real life. It looked like a place built for people who could run faster than humans, stronger than humans, see further than humans.

She didn't say any of that. She just sat on the horse and watched.

They passed through a gate. Guards — armed, alert, clearly trained — stopped them. Victor spoke to them in a voice that was different from the voice he'd used with her. Calmer. More authoritative. More in control. The guards nodded and stepped aside.

Inside, the compound was alive. People were everywhere. Men and women moving with purpose, some carrying tools, some carrying weapons, and some carrying nothing but urgency. They all looked up when Victor and Amelia entered. Some of them stared, some of them whispered, and some looked at her with open hostility.

Amelia kept her head up. She wasn't cowering, she wasn't running. She was sitting on a horse in front of a hundred people who could probably kill her with their bare hands, and she was trying to figure out who she was in this place and why she was here and what was going to happen next.

Victor dismounted first. Then he turned and offered his hand.

She took it. His fingers closed around hers for a second. The bond flared — bright, sudden, undeniable — and she felt it, felt him, felt the way his body tensed when she touched him, the way his breath caught, the way his eyes flicked to her mouth for half a second before he looked away.

Then she let go. She got off the horse, stood in front of him, and she waited.

He took her hand again and led her inside.

---

Victor

He walked her to the main hall. He didn't let go of her hand until they were inside. He didn't know why. He knew why. He just didn't want to admit it.

The hall was large — stone floor, high ceiling, fireplaces on either end. There were people sitting around long tables, eating dinner. They looked up when he entered. The room went quiet. Victor didn't care. He was focused on Amelia — on the way she looked around, on the way her eyes moved from face to face, on the way her body was tense but her posture was straight.

She was scared. He could feel it through the bond — not panic, not terror, but caution, wariness, the careful assessment of someone who didn't know what to trust yet. He felt the need to protect her. It was a physical sensation, like a weight on his chest, like a hand reaching out and squeezing his heart. He hated it. He wanted it. He didn't know what to do with either.

"Perseus," he said.

Perseus was across the room. He was sitting at the table, talking to someone, looking at a map, and the moment Victor entered, he was up. He came over. He looked at Victor. He looked at Amelia. He looked at Victor again.

"You brought a human," he said.

"I brought a witness," Victor said. "A human woman. She lives near the boundary. She saw —" He hesitated. He didn't know what to say. He didn't know how to explain that they hadn't seen anything yet. They'd just looked at each other. They'd just existed in the same space. But that was enough. "She saw something. I need you to question her."

Perseus's eyes narrowed. He looked at Amelia. He was studying her the way he studied everything — calculating, weighing, deciding whether someone was a threat or an asset. It was unsettling to be looked at like that. It was unsettling to be looked at by someone who could read people the way Victor could.

"Come with me," Perseus said to Amelia. "I have questions."

She nodded. She didn't hesitate. She didn't ask Victor for permission. She just looked at Perseus and said, "Sure."

And then she walked away.

Victor watched her go. He couldn't help it. The bond tugged at him — a thin, invisible thread stretching from his chest to hers, pulling him in the direction she moved. He gritted his teeth. He turned to David.

"David," Victor said. "Get over here."

David appeared like he'd been waiting for this. He was leaning against a pillar, arms crossed, grin on his face, and when he saw Amelia walking away with Perseus, his grin got wider.

"You brought home a stray?" David said. "Really, Victor? A stray?"

Victor's voice was flat. It was deadly. "She's not a stray."

David's grin didn't fade. He just shrugged. "I said he brought home a stray. There's a difference."

"You'll watch your mouth," Victor said. "Around her. Around everyone."

David's smile faded. He nodded. "Yeah. Okay."

Victor turned away. He didn't want to deal with David. He didn't want to deal with anything. He wanted to sit down, close his eyes, and think about what had happened today and what it meant and what Josiah was going to tell him when he had a chance to speak to him.

But Josiah was already on his way.

Josiah was moving slowly across the room, leaning on his staff, and when he got to Victor, he didn't look at him. He looked past him. He was watching Amelia. He was watching her talk to Perseus, watching the way Perseus's face was unreadable, watching the way Amelia was standing straight and speaking clearly. Josiah's eyes were old and knowing and unreadable.

"You know her," Josiah said. It wasn't a question.

"No," Victor said. "I don't."

"You do," Josiah said. He turned to Victor and looked at him. His eyes were heavy. His voice was low. "You just didn't know you knew her."

Victor didn't answer. He couldn't. Because Josiah was right.

---

Amelia

Perseus's office was small. Stone walls, wooden desk, a single window looking out over the compound. There were books everywhere — stacks of them on shelves, on the floor, on the desk. She noticed all of that. She noticed the way Perseus closed the door behind them. She noticed the way he sat down and gestured for her to sit.

"Tell me what you saw," Perseus said.

"I saw a man," she said. "Tall, dark hair, gray eyes. He was — " She stopped. She couldn't explain what he was or what he was doing out there at that hour. He had moved through the trees like he owned the shadows, and when he looked at her, it was like he was looking through her skin. She tried to describe it to Perseus, but the words kept slipping away.

"Where?" Perseus said.

"By a stream. Near the northern boundary. He was patrolling. He was — alone."

Perseus was quiet. He was looking at her. He was waiting.

"Did you feel anything unusual?" he asked.

She thought about it. She thought about the tingling in her hands, the pull in her chest, the way her body had reacted to him like it had been waiting for him. She thought about the way the forest had felt different when he was near — not dangerous, just… charged.

"Yes," she said.

"What?"

"I don't know. A sensation. In my hands. In my chest."

Perseus nodded. He wrote something down. He was writing fast, his handwriting tight and precise. He didn't look up.

"How old are you?" he asked.

"Twenty-six."

"Where do you live?"

"Outside the boundary. Near the tree line. I have a cottage. I'm a healer."

He looked up. "A healer. Of humans?"

"Of anyone who needs it. Mostly humans, but — sometimes people come to me in the middle of the night. They don't talk about it, but I treat them anyway."

Perseus was quiet for a long moment. Then he said, "You've treated people who shouldn't be able to survive those kinds of injuries before?"

"Yes."

"How?"

"I don't know. They come to my clinic. They say they fell. Or they got stuck in a thorn bush. Or they were attacked by a dog. But the injuries don't match. So I treat them anyway."

Perseus set his pen down. He looked at her. Really looked at her.

"You're a useful person," he said. "You know what that means?"

"It means I'm not useless."

"It means you might be more important than you realize."

She didn't know what to say to that. She just nodded.

Perseus stood up. "You can go, Victor will tell you what to do next."

She stood up too. She was about to leave when Perseus spoke again.

"One more thing," he said. "When you're with him — when you're near him — do you feel anything else? Anything that doesn't make sense?"

She hesitated. She thought about the tingling. She thought about the pull. She thought about the way her heart had skipped when he touched her waist, the way her body had reacted before her mind even knew what was happening.

"Yes," she said.

"What?"

"I don't know. It's — it's like something inside me woke up. And I don't know what it's waking up to."

Perseus was quiet. He looked at her for a long moment. Then he opened the door and let her out.

---

Amelia

She walked out of the office into the hallway. The compound was alive around her — voices echoing off the stone walls, footsteps, the smell of food, the sound of laughter. It was overwhelming. She didn't know what to do with herself.

She stood in the hallway for a moment. She didn't know where to go. She didn't know who to ask. She didn't know anything.

And then she heard his voice.

"I don't know, Josiah. I really don't."

It was Victor. She didn't know how she knew it was Victor, but she did. The bond pulled her toward it — a thread stretching through the walls, through the air, through the noise of the compound, pulling her toward the sound of his voice.

She followed it.

She found him in a small room near the back of the compound. It was quiet. There was a fireplace. There was a chair. There was Josiah. And there was Victor, standing in front of the fire, his back to her, his hands clenched at his sides.

Josiah turned when she entered. He looked at her. He looked at him. Then, he looked at the two of them together, and he smiled.

"Amelia," he said. "Come in. Sit down."

She didn't know if she should. She looked at Victor. He was still facing the fire. He hadn't turned around. But she could feel him — the way his body tensed when she entered, the way his breathing changed, the way the bond between them flared like a struck match.

She walked in. She sat down.

Josiah looked at Victor. "You know," he said. "We need to talk about this. The bond, the Mate Bond, the Moonlit Rite, and the Moon Goddess. All of it."

Victor didn't answer.

Josiah looked at Amelia. "Amelia," he said. "Do you know what a Mate Bond is?"

She shook her head.

He sat down and leaned forward. His hands were resting on his staff. His eyes were gentle. His voice was patient.

"Let me tell you a story," he said.

And he began.
