# Chapter 2: The Council

The great hall was already half-full when Victor arrived.

He entered through the side door and paused at the threshold, letting the room settle around him. It always took a moment — thirty seconds, maybe a minute — for the murmuring conversations to die down, for heads to turn, for the pack's energy to shift from scattered individuals to something organized and expectant. He was used to it. It didn't make it any easier.

He didn't sit. He stood at the head of the long stone table and let the silence do the work.

David was the first to break it.

"The northern boundary," he said, leaning forward with his elbows on the table. "We need to push forward. If someone's marking our land, we show them we don't take kindly to trespassers."

Perseus shook his head. "That's exactly what they want. They want us to react. If we send a patrol south, they know we're spooked. If we send too many, they know we're scared."

"We're not scared," David snapped.

"No," Victor said quietly. "But we don't need to prove it by walking into a trap."

David's mouth pressed into a thin line. He didn't like being told no — nobody liked it — but he knew better than to push Victor in front of the whole pack. He sat back.

Victor let the quiet settle, then turned to face the room properly. He stood straight, hands resting on the back of the chair at the head of the table, and spoke.

"I'm going to be direct. The boundary markers on the north have been moved again — this time by three feet. Samuel found traces that don't belong to our pack. The scent is old and wrong. And we're missing a hunter."

A murmur rippled through the room. Marcus LaBlanc's name hung in the air like a weight.

"I don't know what happened to him," Victor continued. "I don't know who or what took him. But something is out there. And it's not just testing the boundary — it's testing us."

Josiah Amos sat at Victor's right hand, his weathered face calm, his eyes sharp. He had been Cyrus's advisor. Now he was Victor's. He hadn't said much yet, but his silence carried weight. When he spoke, the room leaned in.

"These things have happened before," he said, his voice low and deliberate. "In my memory, there were signs — animal behavior shifting, the Veil feeling thin, the cold coming at the wrong time of year. Always, it preceded something bigger. Something we didn't see until it was too late."

Emila Sterling, the pack's Omega, sat at the far end of the table. Her warm brown eyes tracked Victor's face as she listened. She was small but unshakeable, with the kind of presence that made people bring her their problems and stay for the answers. The pack's healer. Its emotional center. The most politically astute person in the room, though she'd never claim it.

Josiah caught her eye. She gave a small nod — the same nod Cyrus had given him, a thousand times, in a thousand meetings. The nod that meant *I hear you, and I don't want to hear it.*

"The Veil." Perseus's expression hardened. He shook his head slowly. "The Veil doesn't just *thin*. It's been holding for centuries. What exactly are you saying happened?"

"I'm saying the pack has felt the thinning for weeks," Victor said. "I can feel it. It's like standing in a room where the air pressure drops right before a storm. You know something's coming. You just don't know what."

David frowned. "The Veil is the Moon's doing. She maintains it. If it's thinning, it's because she's allowing it."

"Maybe," Josiah said. "Or maybe it's thinning because something is pulling at it from the other side."

The room went quiet. That was the wrong thing to say. Everyone in the room knew what was on the other side of the Veil. That was why the Veil existed.

Emila cleared her throat. "Before we spiral into fear," she said, her voice even and measured, "let's focus on what we can control. We have a missing hunter. We have boundary disturbances. We have a pack that's nervous. We need to address those things, not the ghosts we think might be coming."

Victor nodded at her. "Agreed. Here's what we're doing. Patrols on the northern boundary are expanding immediately — double the schedule, triple the watchers. No one goes out past the markers alone. Samuel, you're leading the north sector. I want boots on the ground around the clock."

Samuel LaBlanc, Victor's best tracker, nodded. His jaw was tight, his hands clenched in fists at his sides. His brother was out there somewhere. He wasn't going to rest until he was found. "Understood."

"Perseus, reinforce the boundary stones with fresh cairns. Mark them with pack sigils. If someone's walking our territory, I want them to know we're watching."

Perseus inclined his head. "Consider it done."

"And David—" Victor turned to him. David's hands were still clenched on his knees, his eyes bright with the urge to act. "You're on the south side. Monitor the Hanley border. I don't want conflict, but I also don't want us blindsided. Keep your men sharp. Keep your head cool."

David's jaw worked. "Cool isn't going to be easy with Hanley pressing on our southern flank."

"I know."

David exhaled, frustrated. He wanted to fight. They all did, deep down. Shifters were predators by nature, and predators didn't like not knowing where the next meal was coming from. But Victor had been Alpha long enough to know that not every problem could be solved with teeth.

Josiah stood. His chair scraped against the stone floor, and the sound cut through the room like a bell. He was older than anyone else at the table — older than Cyrus, probably older than any of them would be — and when he stood, people moved.

"The old ones said the dark would return," he said, looking at the entire room, not just Victor. "I thought they were just stories. Stories meant to frighten pups. But stories that survive this long aren't just stories. They're warnings. And warnings don't come without reason."

He looked at Victor. "We need to prepare for something we can't fight with swords and teeth."

Victor met his gaze. "Then tell us what to prepare for."

Josiah's expression didn't change. But something in his eyes softened — the way it always did when he was about to say something that didn't fit in a pack meeting.

"Not today," he said. "Today, we hold the line. Tomorrow, we think."

Victor didn't press. Josiah had never been wrong about timing. Not once.

He looked back at the room. "Any other concerns?"

Silence.

"Good. Dismissed."

The pack members filed out, talking quietly, faces tight with concern. Marco clapped David on the shoulder as he passed, and David shrugged him off. Becca lingered by the door, looking at her brother, looking at Victor, not sure whether to stay or go.

Emila found Victor at the head of the table after everyone else had left.

"You haven't eaten," she said.

"I'm fine."

"Victor." She used his name — not Alpha, not sir — and he looked up to find her standing in front of him with a plate of food. Roast beef, bread, vegetables. Homemade. Her way of caring.

"I'm not—"

"Sit down." She didn't raise her voice. She didn't need to. Emila was Omega. When she said *sit down*, the pack sat down.

He sat.

She set the plate in front of him and leaned against the table, watching him eat. "You're carrying it alone again."

"I'm fine."

"You're not." Her voice was soft but firm. "You're the Alpha. You think that means you have to be strong all the time. You think that means you can't show fear. But you're not a damn wall, Victor. You're a person. And people aren't built to carry everything by themselves."

He looked up. Her eyes were kind, but there was steel underneath. Emila knew him better than anyone in the pack. She had been there since he was a pup — since before Cyrus died, before the Alpha bond shifted to him. She had seen him fall. She had seen him get up. She had also seen him bleed.

"I'm not—"

"Save it." She pushed the plate closer. "Eat. Then we'll talk about what you're not telling me."

He ate. He didn't finish it. But he ate enough.

When she left, he sat alone in the great hall as the sun went down and the fire crackled low. He could hear the pack moving through the compound — boots on stone, voices in the distance, the low murmur of lives lived together for centuries. He was surrounded by family.

And he had never felt more alone.

---

The balcony was small — one of the few things in the compound that wasn't designed to impress. It jutted from the second-floor wall, barely wide enough for two people to stand side by side, overlooking the valley and the darkening tree line beyond. Victor had found it by accident three years ago, the night after Cyrus's funeral, when he'd climbed out the window and stood there in the snow until his feet went numb.

He came up here now to think. To breathe. To pretend, for a few minutes, that he wasn't the Alpha.

The sky was dark. The first stars were visible, faint pinpricks against the overcast blanket. The moon wasn't out yet — not for a few more hours. But even without it, he could feel it. The pull. Like a thread running from his chest up into the sky, tugging at something he couldn't name.

He stared north.

The forest stretched for miles — dark, quiet, wrong. He could feel the pressure pressing against the edge of his awareness, like a hand on glass. If he squinted, he could almost see it: a faint shimmer, like heat rising from asphalt, except this wasn't heat. It was absence. A place where the world thinned to nothing.

And beneath it all, beneath the pressure and the wrongness, beneath the fear and the weight of a pack's survival on his shoulders, there was something else. A sound. Not a real sound — there was nothing in the air to create it. But it was there, deep in his bones, in the part of him that had been wolf before it had been human, older than language and older than memory.

The moon was calling.

He didn't know why. He didn't know what it wanted. But for the first time in three years, he felt something that wasn't the weight. Something that wasn't the fear.

Something that felt, for just a moment, like purpose.
