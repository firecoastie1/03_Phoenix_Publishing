# Chapter 3: Amelia's Day

The morning started the way mornings always did — with silence. Not the deep, empty silence of a house where nobody lived, but the quiet hum of a place that was entirely hers. Sunlight came through the kitchen window in a slanted rectangle, catching dust motes that drifted like slow snow. The kettle sat on the stove, the stove sat on the counter, the counter sat next to a window that looked out at the tree line and beyond that, the edge of a forest nobody in town talked about unless they were drunk.

Amelia Wren stood at that counter with a chipped blue mug in her hand and the kind of stillness that came from living alone long enough to appreciate it.

Her cat, a scarred slate-black cat named Luna with orange eyes who looked permanently unimpressed by everything, jumped onto the counter and stared at her. She set the mug down and opened a tin of fish. Luna ate like she was doing her a favor.

She poured tea, carried it to the table, and sat. On her left wrist, half-hidden under the cuff of her sweater sleeve, a crescent moon tattoo pressed against her skin. Inked by her grandmother when she was twelve — *"so you remember that you come from someone who knows how to read the sky."* Amelia had been fifteen when her grandmother died. She was twenty-six now. The tattoo was the only thing she had left of her that felt sacred.

She drank her tea. She ate toast. She looked at the window. The trees were still. Too still for this time of morning — the birds should have been singing by now, but the forest had been quiet for weeks. Not peaceful quiet. Holding-its-breath quiet. The kind of silence that made the hair on the back of her neck want to stand up.

She told herself it was nothing.

---

The clinic wasn't much — a small stone building on the edge of town, the kind of place that looked like it had always been there even though Amelia had only opened it six months ago. The sign out front said *Amelia Wren — Herbalist & Healer* in letters her grandmother had painted decades ago, and below that, in smaller script, *By Appointment, or If You're Bleeding, Come Anyway.*

She didn't need to tell anyone she was open. People knew. Word traveled through this part of the county faster than cell service ever could, and word was that Amelia Wren could fix what the doctor couldn't — or wouldn't.

The first patient of the day was Mrs. Gable, a sixty-year-old with arthritis in her hands and stubbornness in her spine. She came in with a tin of homemade cookies and a complaint that the weather was making her knuckles ache. Amelia made her a salve from comfrey and wintergreen, rubbed it into her hands with firm, practiced fingers, and sent her off with two tablets of willow bark for the pain. Mrs. Gable hugged her on the way out.

"Your grandmother would be proud," she said.

Amelia smiled. "She'd tell you to stop leaning on your right side when you walk."

Mrs. Gable laughed. "She always did see too much."

After Mrs. Gable left, the second patient arrived. He was a young hunter — maybe twenty-two, lean and windburned, with a gash on his forearm that was definitely not from a tree branch. The edges were too smooth. The shape was wrong. He said he'd fallen on a rock. She'd heard that story three times this week.

She cleaned the wound, packed it with honey and yarrow, wrapped it tightly. He winced but didn't pull away. When she was done, he pressed a five-dollar bill into her hand and muttered a thank-you that sounded more like a prayer than a word.

"What happened out there?" she asked quietly.

He looked up. His eyes were wide, dark, and young. "Nothing. Just... lost my footing. It's nothing."

She didn't push. She'd learned that lesson early — some people didn't want answers. They just wanted the bleeding to stop so they could go back to pretending everything was normal.

She watched him leave through the window. He walked fast, eyes on the ground, shoulders hunched like he was carrying something heavier than his pack. He looked back once at the tree line behind him, like he expected something to be there. He was probably right to.

Amelia washed her hands, locked the front door, and sat down at her desk. She opened a notebook — blank pages waiting for her own notes, her own remedies, her own thoughts. She didn't write in it much. Most of what she knew lived in her hands, in the feel of a pulse under her fingers, in the way a wound looked and smelled and bled. But sometimes — usually at night, when the house was quiet and Luna was curled on her feet and the silence felt less like peace and more like absence — she wrote things down.

Today she wrote: *Three hunters with strange wounds in one week. All from the north side of the boundary. All saying it was an accident. None of them talking about what they saw.*

She closed the notebook. She didn't read it again. She didn't need to. She remembered everything.

---

The afternoon stretched. Two more patients — a broken finger, a sunburn. She tended to both. She ate lunch standing over the sink, a bowl of soup that her neighbor had dropped off. She drank water. She looked out the window every ten minutes, even though there was nothing to see. The sun was low, the light was turning gold, and the forest was still holding its breath.

She was wiping down the counter when the bell above the door chimed.

She turned.

A woman in her late sixties, gray hair pinned up, carrying a canvas bag. She didn't look like a patient. She looked like a person who brought things.

"Evening, Mrs. Calloway," Amelia said.

Mrs. Calloway set the bag on the counter. "Thought you might be hungry. You always look peaky by afternoon."

Amelia smiled despite herself. "You always bring me soup."

"You always need soup." Mrs. Calloway's eyes twinkled. She reached into the bag and pulled out a small packet wrapped in paper. "And I brought you something else. Your grandmother gave this to me before she passed. Said if I ever saw someone who needed it, I should give it to them."

Amelia took the packet. It was heavy, warm, wrapped in twine. She didn't open it. She didn't need to — the paper smelled like fresh pancakes, bacon, and fried eggs, and something she couldn't name but recognized in her bones.

"Tell me you didn't give this to anyone else," she said.

Mrs. Calloway shook her head. "Just you. And only because the time was right."

Amelia's throat felt tight. "I don't— I don't know what to say."

"Say you'll take care of yourself," Mrs. Calloway said. She reached across the counter and squeezed Amelia's wrist, right over the moon tattoo. "You're not as invisible as you think you are, child."

After Mrs. Calloway left, Amelia sat in the empty clinic and held the packet without opening it. She stared at her hands. They were steady. They had always been steady. Her grandmother taught her to hold a scalpel with her pinky finger raised and her thumb anchored. She taught her to read a pulse like it was a language. She taught her that healing wasn't just about fixing what was broken — it was about understanding what had caused the break in the first place.

She didn't understand what was causing the breaks now.

She put the packet in her pocket. She stood up. She locked the door behind her and walked home.

---

Her cottage was small — one story, stone walls, a thatched roof, a garden out back that was more weeds than herbs because she'd been meaning to get to it. It sat on the edge of the woods, just far enough away that people didn't think she was strange for living there. Close enough that she could hear the forest at night.

She let Luna in first, locked the door, and set the kettle on. She changed out of her work clothes into a soft cotton shirt and loose pants, made tea, and ate a bowl of broth. She sat at her small kitchen table and let her hands rest on the wood grain, feeling the warmth of the cup through her palms.

The house was quiet. It was always quiet. Not the kind of quiet that meant peace — the kind that meant emptiness. A space where somebody else's footsteps should echo. A voice that should answer back. A hand that should reach across the table and touch hers.

She didn't think about it much. She'd learned, over the years, to just sit with the silence and let it be what it was. It was calmer that way. Less painful.

Her grandmother had tried to tell her she wasn't built for it. *"You feel too deeply, Amelia. That's not a flaw. That's a gift. But if you keep bottling it up, it'll eat you alive."*

She'd spent her whole life being told she was too much. Too intense. Too emotional. Too sensitive. Too intense about her work, too sensitive about her feelings, too much of everything for people who wanted less. Sometimes — late at night, when she was walking home through the dark — she'd catch figures at the edge of the woods. Just shapes, barely visible. They never spoke to her. They just watched. Like she was something they were trying to figure out.

So she'd learned to live apart. She'd learned to make herself small. She'd learned to be competent and self-reliant and quiet, and to let people decide for themselves whether she was interesting enough to talk to. Most of them decided she wasn't.

It is fine.

You are fine.

Everything is fine.

She told herself it was fine a lot. Sometimes she wondered if it was enough.

---

The kettle whistled. She poured another cup. She carried it to the window and stood there, looking out at the trees.

The moon was out. A sliver, pale and thin, just beginning to rise behind the ridge. She watched it for a long moment, letting the quiet settle around her. The forest was still quiet. Luna had jumped onto the table and was watching her with the same orange eyes she'd worn since she was old enough to feed her.

She raised her mug in a silent toast. "To another day," she said.

Luna blinked slowly. It was the closest thing to agreement she'd get.

She set her mug down and turned away from the window. She was about to head to the bedroom when it hit her.

A sharp, sudden pull in her chest. Like something had hooked into her heart and yanked.

She froze. Her breath caught. Her hands — her hands started tingling, a strange, electric sensation that crawled up from her fingertips and spread through her arms like ice water.

She gripped the edge of the table. Her eyes went wide.

"What—"

It wasn't pain. It wasn't anything she could name. It was a physical sensation, like gravity had shifted and everything in her body was tilting toward something she couldn't see. She looked around the room. The same room she'd been standing in three seconds ago. The same table. The same window. The same trees outside.

But the pull didn't stop. It didn't even ease.

It got stronger.

Her chest ached. Her hands vibrated. Her heart hammered against her ribs like it was trying to get out, or get somewhere, or to someone.

She took a step forward. Then another. She didn't know where she was going. She just knew that standing still was worse than whatever this was.

She walked to the door. She opened it. The night air hit her face, cool and clean and smelling of pine and something else — something she couldn't name but recognized in the same way she recognized the tingling in her hands: like a feeling she'd had before, long ago, in a dream, and couldn't place.

She stepped onto the porch.

The pull didn't change. It didn't point her toward the cottage, toward the garden, toward town. It pointed her toward the forest. Toward the boundary. Toward the dark trees that held the edge of the world.

And for the first time in her life, Amelia Wren felt something that wasn't loneliness.

It was a hook. It was a thread. It was a pull in her chest that was stronger than any fear, any instinct, any rational thought.

She told herself it was nothing.

She told herself it was nerves; it was exhaustion; it was the weather.

She stood on her porch, her hands shaking, her heart racing, and watched the trees in the dark, wondering why something she couldn't name felt more real than anything she'd ever known.
