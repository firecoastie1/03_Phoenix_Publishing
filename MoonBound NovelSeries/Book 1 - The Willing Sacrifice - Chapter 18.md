# Chapter 18: The First Date (Sort Of)

Amelia

The kitchen at dawn smelled like burnt toast and bad decisions.

She couldn't sleep. The bond hummed in her chest, pulling her toward the compound, so she walked over, wrapped in a coat, and found herself in the kitchen at five. She had no excuse. No reason. Just a pull she couldn't ignore. Now it was morning, and the tea was cold and she was standing here, and the bond was humming louder than ever.

She stood at the counter with a kettle she didn't need to boil, a mug she didn't need to fill, and a cup of tea that had gone cold three minutes ago. The compound's kitchen was too clean, too organized, too much like a place someone who didn't cook came to make other people feel welcome. There were knives on a magnetic strip, perfectly aligned. Spices in labeled jars. A stand mixer she would probably never use.

The back door opened.

She didn't turn around right away. She listened to his footsteps, heavy and deliberate, but quieter than they would be during the day. He stopped three paces behind her. She could feel the bond pull, felt it like heat on the back of her neck.

"Morning," he said.

"Morning."

He was quiet for a long moment. She could feel him watching her. She could also feel that he was trying not to look like he was watching her, which made it worse somehow.

"Is that tea cold?" he asked.

She turned. He was wearing a dark t-shirt and a pair of jeans that had seen better days, his hair still sleep-tousled, his gray eyes the color of storm clouds and probably a dozen other things she was not supposed to be thinking about at six in the morning. He looked like a man who had not slept. She looked like a woman who had not slept. That should have been a joke. It was not a joke.

"Three minutes ago," she said.

He rubbed the back of his neck. "I come down here sometimes. Before the day starts. I don't know why. It's just quiet."

"I like quiet," she said.

He nodded. He leaned against the far counter, arms crossed, and she watched him do it, the way his shoulders relaxed when he wasn't giving orders, the way his jaw unclenched when he wasn't managing a pack. He looked different when no one was watching. Softer. Less guarded. It made something in her chest ache, and not because of the bond.

"You don't have to stay in the guest quarters," he said.

She blinked. The tea, the cold kitchen, the bad timing, none of it mattered. This was the wrong place to have this conversation. But she was not going to pretend it wasn't happening.

"I don't have to stay where?"

"In the guest quarters. There's a room. The last one was used for storage. It's clean. The bed is fine. You don't have to — I'm not asking you to move in. I'm just saying. If you wanted to. There's a room."

She stared at him. He was looking at the wall now, his jaw tight, like he was already regretting saying it out loud. He had offered her a room in his compound and made it sound like he was apologizing for it.

"That's very generous," she said. "But I need to think about Luna."

Victor's eyes went to hers immediately. Not the tea. Not the room. Luna.

"Your cat."

"She has a name. And she's not a pet. She's a cat." She set the mug down on the counter and turned to face him fully. "She stays where she's safe. If I move to the compound, she comes with me. If she doesn't come, I don't go."

Victor was quiet. He studied her face like he was trying to read something he couldn't quite see. Then he nodded.

"She comes," he said. "Of course she comes."

She waited. That was it? Of course she comes?

"Thank you," she said, and he looked surprised, like he hadn't expected gratitude. Or maybe he had, and he just didn't know what to do with it.

"It's just a room," he said.

"It's not just a room," she said. "But I'll think about it. Okay?"

He nodded again. "Okay."

They stood in the kitchen for a moment, neither of them saying anything, the bond humming between them like a second heartbeat. Then she spoke.

"What's it like?" she asked. "Being the Alpha?"

He looked at her, really looked at her, and something shifted. He wasn't the Alpha right now. He was just Victor.

"It's heavy," he said. "It's standing in a room full of people who depend on you for their safety and knowing that every decision you make could save them or kill them. It's feeling like the weight of the pack sits on your shoulders like a stone and you can't put it down because they need you to carry it. It's knowing that if you make the wrong call, people die. It's —"

He stopped. He took a breath. He looked at the wall again.

"My father built the Blackwood Pack into something strong. He kept them alive through three winters, through two wars, through everything. He died protecting them."

Amelia didn't speak. She just waited.

"I carry his legacy like a shield," he said. "I hold everything together with my own hands. Tradition. Strength. Order. But sometimes I look at the map and I can see the gaps. The thin places. And I know the old ways aren't enough anymore. I just don't know what is."

She set her hands flat on the counter. She was a healer. She knew how to measure before she touched. This was no different.

"Maybe the answer isn't a new way," she said. "Maybe it's just letting other people carry the weight for a while."

He looked at her. His eyes were gray, storm-cloud gray, unreadable. He looked like a man who had not expected someone to understand him without speaking a word he had already said.

"Amelia," he said.

She was close to him now. Not deliberately. She hadn't moved. He had, or she had, or the bond had pulled them together without either of them noticing. The space between them was small. The bond was loud. She could feel his presence like heat, like a note held in the air, like everything she had been waiting for without knowing it.

He didn't step back.

She didn't step back.

His eyes dropped to her mouth. Hers dropped to his.

The bond flared.

It was like a wire snapping taut, like a current running through her veins, like everything in her body was reaching for him and he was reaching for her and they were both going to collide.

The back door banged open.

"Alpha — David said you'd be down here —" Marco stood in the doorway with a stack of supply reports in his arms, his eyes wide, his mouth open, and he looked at them both like he had just walked into something he was not supposed to see.

Victor stepped back so fast he nearly tripped over the counter. Amelia felt the bond break like a snapped wire and the loss hit her like a physical blow.

"Marco," Victor said, and his voice was steady, but it was too steady, like he was holding himself together by force.

"Sorry," Marco said. He looked at the stack of reports in his arms like they were suddenly very interesting. "Sorry. I'll come back later."

He backed out of the kitchen. The door closed. The silence that followed was worse than the interruption.

Amelia looked at Victor. Victor looked at the wall. The bond hummed between them, low and steady, like a promise neither of them was ready to keep.

---

Evening came. The sky turned the color of bruised plums, and Amelia stood on the roof of her cottage, watching the sun sink behind the ridge, and the bond between her and Victor hummed like a second heartbeat in her chest.

She had not meant to come here. She had meant to sit at her kitchen table and read her notes and pretend she was fine and think about a room in the compound and a cat who slept on her feet and a man who offered her a room like he was apologizing for it. But the bond pulled, and she followed it, and now she was sitting on her roof, waiting.

He arrived five minutes later.

He didn't knock. He didn't call out. He just climbed the stairs and walked onto the roof and sat down three feet from her, close enough that she could feel his warmth, far enough that she didn't have to look at him.

They sat in silence for a long time. The bond was steady between them, warm and present, like a wire pulled taut but not broken.

Amelia closed her eyes. She let her hands rest on her knees. She let her breathing slow. She thought about trust. She thought about how much she trusted him, not with the bond, not with anything magical or cosmic or fated, but with the small, human things. The way he had said her cat's name without hesitation. The way he had looked at the wall when he talked about his father. The way he had looked at her mouth and not looked away until the world interrupted.

She pushed something through the bond.

Not a word. Not an image. A feeling. Trust. The warm, steady kind. The kind that doesn't ask for anything in return.

The bond carried it. She could feel it travel, like a thread pulling taut, like a note finding its frequency, and then Victor stiffened. His breath caught. She felt it through the bond, felt him catch, felt the exact moment his guard slipped.

Then he let out a breath he didn't know he was holding.

He pushed back.

It hit her like a wave. Protectiveness, fierce and raw, the kind of protectiveness that wasn't about control or ownership but about the deep, animal need to keep someone safe. It was so intense it made her chest ache. It was the feeling of a man who had spent his whole life carrying weight for other people and finally found something he wanted to carry for himself.

She didn't say anything. She couldn't. She just let it wash over her and let herself feel it and let herself understand, for the first time, what the bond was really for.

It wasn't magic. It wasn't a tool. It was a bridge. Two people, standing on opposite sides of something too big for either of them alone, reaching across and finding the other person's hand.

They sat on the roof until the stars came out. Neither of them said anything. They just sat there, two people who were suddenly, terrifyingly close.

And when she went back inside, she didn't close her eyes for the first time in weeks and think about being alone.
