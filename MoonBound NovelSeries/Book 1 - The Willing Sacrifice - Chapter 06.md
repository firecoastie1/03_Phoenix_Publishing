# Chapter 6: The Missing Hunter

Marcus LaBlanc was gone.

Not injured. Not captured. Not anywhere anyone could find him.

Marcus LaBlanc was twenty-two, a young hunter who'd been assigned to patrol the northern boundary at dawn. His patrol report was the last thing he left behind — three lines of neat handwriting on the supply room clipboard, noting nothing out of the ordinary. The markers were standing. The trail was clear. The scent was fresh, leading north, and then it just ended.

No tracks after that. No blood. No struggle.

Just silence.

---

The emergency council was called before noon.

It was the third one in as many days. Victor stood at the head of the long wooden table, his hands flat against the grain, his jaw set, his eyes fixed on the map laid out before him. The northern boundary stretched along the edge of the map like a thin red line, and on that line, at a point marked by a small wooden post, someone had drawn a circle.

Marcus's patrol report had been the last thing recorded before his scent trail ended. No one knew what had happened after that. No one knew where he was.

David was already pacing.

"We need to send a team out," he said. "Right now. He's probably hurt — maybe he wandered off the trail, maybe he's unconscious. We need to find him before nightfall."

Perseus sat at the far end of the table, his expression unreadable. He'd been quiet this whole time. But he wasn't ignoring it. He was watching — watching Victor, watching David, watching the way the pack shifted around the table, waiting for him to make a decision.

Perseus knew what was at stake. He'd learned the hard way in his first year as a hunter, when he'd made a mistake that cost a friend his life, that rushing into the unknown was a fast way to die. And he wasn't going to make the same mistake twice.

"We don't know what we're dealing with," he said. His voice was calm. Quiet. But the room went still when he spoke. "No tracks. No blood. No sign of a struggle. This isn't an animal attack. This isn't an accident. Something took him. And we don't know what it is."

David stopped pacing. He looked at Perseus. "Then we send a team to find out."

"We send a team," Perseus said, "and we all end up missing. Or worse."

The room went quiet.

Perseus looked at Victor. He thought of Josiah — sitting in the corner of the great hall, listening to the stories the young ones told and pretending not to care. The old hunter had said it once, years ago, when no one was paying attention: *"The old ones said the dark would return. Stories meant to frighten pups. But stories that survive this long aren't just stories. They're warnings."*

Perseus had dismissed it then. He wasn't sure he could dismiss it now.

"I'm ordering a full search," Victor said. His voice was steady. Firm. Commanding. "But David — no one goes alone. Two hunters minimum. Three if we can spare them. And they don't go past the boundary markers. They follow Marcus's scent to the last point and they come back."

David opened his mouth to argue. He closed it and nodded. He was disappointed, but he understood.

Perseus watched him go. He looked at Victor. He didn't say anything. But his look said everything.

*You're going to need more than a search party.*

---

Amelia

Amelia heard about the missing hunter the way you heard about things in a small town — not through official announcements, but through whispers in the market, through patients who came in with rumors, the way people looked at her when they thought she wasn't paying attention.

Mrs. Calloway mentioned it first. She came in for her blood pressure refill, stood at the counter, and said it casually, like she was talking about the weather.

"Pack's nervous," she said. "One of their hunters gone missing. Out past the northern boundary."

Amelia nodded. She didn't say anything. She handed Mrs. Calloway her prescription, smiled, and went back to work.

But she didn't forget it.

By afternoon, she'd heard it from three more patients. All different stories. One said he'd been found lying in a field, unconscious. Another said his horse had come back without him. A third said he'd been taken. Taken. Not lost. Taken.

Amelia didn't say anything. But she listened. And she remembered.

That night, she sat at her kitchen table. Luna slept on the windowsill. The forest was quiet. The pull in her chest was there again, faint but persistent, like a clock ticking in another room. Her hands were tingling. She told herself it was nothing.

It wasn't nothing.

---

Victor

Victor rode the northern boundary at dusk.

He was alone. It was against his orders — he'd told the others not to go alone — but he was the Alpha, and the Alpha didn't follow his own rules when it mattered. He was the best tracker in the pack. He was the one who could find a scent in a storm, who could read a trail like a book, who could smell fear on a man three miles away.

He needed to find Marcus.

He followed the scent trail from the supply room clipboard to the last marker. Marcus's scent was fresh — too fresh. It led north, through the trees, through the underbrush, and then it ended. Just like that. No trace. No disturbance. Just silence.

Victor dismounted. He ran his hands over the ground. No drag marks. No blood. No signs of a struggle. He closed his eyes. He breathed in. The scent trail just stopped. It didn't fade. It didn't trail off. It just ended, like a thread cut cleanly with a pair of scissors.

He didn't like it.

He didn't like the way the forest looked around him — the trees, tall and dark, their branches weaving together like hands reaching over his head. The way the fog was starting to roll in, low and gray, creeping between the trunks like something alive. The way the silence felt heavy, like the whole forest was holding its breath, waiting for something to happen.

He didn't like it.

But he also felt it — the pull in his chest, sharp and insistent, pointing north, toward whatever had taken Marcus, toward whatever was waiting in the dark.

He stood there for a long time. He thought about going further. He thought about following the pull, following whatever it was that was pulling at him like a thread, into the dark, into the fog, into whatever was waiting.

He didn't go.

He got back on his horse. He rode back to the compound. And he told himself he was fine.

It wasn't fine.
