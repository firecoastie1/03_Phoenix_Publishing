# Chapter 7: The Forest

Amelia

Something was wrong with the woods. Not wrong in a way she could name. Not a sick tree or a dead animal or a broken branch. Wrong in the way you know something's wrong when the silence feels different, when the birds don't sing where they should, when the air smells like nothing at all and that terrifies you more than a bad smell would.

Amelia knew the woods. She'd spent her whole life near them, walking through them, gathering herbs in them, knowing every path and every clearing and every place where the light fell through the canopy in a particular way. She knew what a healthy forest sounded like.

This wasn't healthy.

She'd been thinking about it for days. Thinking, wondering, trying to put her finger on something she couldn't quite name. She told herself it was nothing. It was the season. It was the weather. It was nerves from the rumors people were spreading.

But her hands were still tingling.

Every morning when she woke up, her fingers felt like they'd fallen asleep, and every night when she lay down, her chest ached with that same pulling sensation — steady, insistent, pointing north, toward the tree line, toward the boundary of the land she knew and the land she didn't.

She told herself it was curiosity.

It wasn't curiosity.

---

The rumors had started after the missing hunter. That much she knew — or at least, that much she'd heard people talking about at the market, at the general store, at the post office. People were nervous. They talked in hushed tones when they thought she wasn't listening. They talked louder when they thought she was.

One woman said there had been lights in the woods, strange, flickering lights that moved in patterns that didn't make sense. Another said a farmer's dog had gone out one morning and come back the next, trembling, with its fur covered in frost even though the weather had been warm. A third said she'd seen something in the trees, something tall and dark that wasn't there when she looked again.

Amelia didn't believe in ghosts. She didn't believe in monsters. But she was a healer, and healers knew what real injuries looked like. She'd seen enough strange wounds in her time to know that not everything that hurt you came from a tree branch or a fall or a fever.

Some things came from somewhere else entirely.

That night, she sat at her kitchen table and made a decision.

She would go to the boundary. She would investigate. She would find out what was going on.

She wasn't a foolish woman. She knew the risks. She packed supplies — dried food, water, a lantern, matches, a knife, bandages, herbs. She told Luna to stay home. She locked the cottage door and walked north.

The tingling in her hands got stronger the further she went.

By the time she reached the edge of the tree line, her fingers were vibrating. She stopped. She looked at them — pale, ordinary, scarred from a thousand small cuts and burns. She didn't understand why they were tingling. She didn't understand why her chest felt tight. She didn't understand why the pull in her bones felt like something reaching for her, like a hand stretching across the dark, trying to find something it couldn't name.

She told herself it was the cold.

It wasn't the cold.

She walked into the forest.

---

The deeper she went, the stranger it got.

The air felt thick — not humid, not heavy with rain, but dense, like standing too close to something that was humming. A low vibration that she could feel in her skin but not hear with her ears. It was the kind of feeling that made the back of her neck prickle, that made her want to run, but also, made her want to stay.

She couldn't explain it.

The trees were tall — older than she'd ever seen them. Their trunks wider, their branches higher, and their leaves a darker green than anything should have been. The forest smelled different here, too. Not like pine or damp earth or the sweet decay of fallen leaves. It smelled like something else entirely — something metallic, sharp, almost electric, like the air before a storm.

She didn't understand it. But she kept walking.

By evening, the light had changed. The canopy had closed overhead, and the forest floor was dim, even though the sky was clear. Shadows moved between the trees, not just the ordinary shadows of branches swaying in the wind, but wrong, like they were flickering in and out of sight, like they were stepping through something she couldn't see.

She turned toward them.

They stopped.

She couldn't see them. Not really. She could see their outlines — dark shapes against the darker background of the forest — but when she tried to focus on them, they vanished. Just like that. Gone. As if they'd never been there at all.

But she could feel them.

She could feel them looking at her.

It wasn't fear. Not exactly. It was worse than fear, it was the knowledge that something was watching her, something that existed in a space she couldn't perceive, something that was aware of her presence and was studying her in return. She could feel it the way you feel someone looking at your back when you're walking down a dark street. You can't see them. You know they're there. And that knowledge sits in your chest like a stone.

She stood very still.

The shadows didn't move.

She didn't either.

After a long time, she turned around and walked away. She didn't run. She didn't look back. She just kept walking, faster and faster, until she reached the edge of the tree line and could see the sky again.

She stopped. She breathed. She turned around and looked at the forest.

It looked normal. Just trees. Just shadows. Just quiet.

She didn't know what she'd seen. She didn't know what she'd felt. But she knew, with a certainty that sat deep in her bones, that something was out there. Something on the other side of something she couldn't name. Something that was watching.

She didn't go back that night.

She set up camp a short distance from the boundary, close enough to see the tree line, far enough that the shadows didn't follow her. She built a small fire, ate a piece of dried bread, then sat on her pack and listened to the forest and waited for the sun to go down.

The tingling in her hands didn't stop.

It never stopped.

---

That night, she dreamed of the forest.

Not the same forest, not the one she'd walked through during the day. This forest was darker, deeper, filled with trees that stretched up into nothing, their branches twisting and turning like hands reaching for something they'd never touch. The air was thick with mist, gray, cold, alive.

And in the mist, she saw them.

Shadows. Tall, dark, shapeless shadows that moved through the trees like smoke, flickering in and out of sight. They weren't real. She knew they weren't real. But they were watching her. They were looking at her with something that might have been hunger, might have been curiosity, might have been something she didn't have a name for.

She woke.

Her hands were tingling.

The fire was burning low.

The forest was quiet.

And somewhere in the dark, beyond the tree line, something was watching her.
