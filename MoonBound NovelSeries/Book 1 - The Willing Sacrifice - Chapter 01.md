# Chapter 1: The Alpha's Burden

The boundary markers had been moved again.

Victor knelt in the damp soil at the northern edge of Blackwood territory, running his fingers along the half-buried stone that marked the western perimeter. It sat three feet east of where it should have been. Three feet. To anyone else, nothing. To an Alpha, it was a declaration.

He stood and wiped his hands on his jeans, scanning the tree line. Douglas firs rose on all sides — ancient, dense, their needles sharp against the overcast sky. The forest here was thick enough to swallow sound. Wolves had grown up in this country. Shifted in it. Died in it. The land knew them the way a mother knows a child's footsteps — even in the dark.

Not this time.

Something was walking the boundary. Something that didn't belong.

"Alpha."

Victor didn't turn. He knew the voice — Samuel LaBlanc, the pack's best tracker. Quiet, methodical, and loyal to a fault. The kind of warrior who would take a blade for the pack without blinking.

"Report," Victor said.

Samuel approached through the brush, his dark hair damp with mist, eyes already scanning the tree line. "I picked up traces an hour ago. Wolf — but not ours. Not Blackwood. Someone's been marking our territory, but they're not doing it from the outside."

Victor's jaw tightened. "From the inside."

"Yeah. Whoever did this knew the patrol routes. They walked them." Samuel hesitated. "I also caught a scent that isn't wolf. It's old. Wrong. Like… standing next to a grave that's been dug up."

Victor didn't answer. He didn't need to. He could feel it — a pressure at the edge of perception, like a room slowly filling with water and no one willing to name it.

"What about the missing hunter?" Victor asked.

Samuel's face darkened. "No sign of Marcus. No tracks leading out. Nothing. He was on the northern boundary at dawn. Radio went silent at twelve-thirteen. No body, no blood, no—"

Victor held up a hand. He didn't need the rest. Marcus was one of his lieutenants. A good warrior. Loyal. The kind of man who laughed too loud and fed strays from his pocket. If he was gone, it meant one of two things: something ancient and hungry had taken him, or something else entirely had.

Neither option was acceptable.

"Mark the boundary with fresh cairns," Victor said. "Double the patrol schedule. I want eyes on the northern sector around the clock. No one goes out past the markers alone."

"Victor—" Samuel started, then caught himself. He'd only called Victor by his first name a handful of times in the five years he'd served the pack. It was a habit born of urgency, not insolence. "David's already pushing for a counter-patrol. He wants to go south and see if anyone's been crossing from the Hanley side."

Victor exhaled slowly. The Hanley pack. Another wolf faction, three hundred miles south in Louisiana swampland, but wolves were nomads by instinct and the boundary between their territory and Blackwood's was a negotiation that never ended. If the Hanleys were testing the northern perimeter, they were looking for weakness. And Victor had to decide whether to show strength or hide it.

"No," he said. "Not yet. We don't know who's moving or where. If we send a counter-patrol south, we leave the north exposed. I'd rather look stupid than vulnerable."

Samuel nodded. "Understood."

He hesitated, then added, "The pack is asking questions. Josiah says you haven't slept in three nights. Emila says you haven't eaten properly in four. They're worried."

Victor almost smiled. Almost. "Tell Emila I'm fine. Tell Josiah I'm not dying. And tell the pack that Alpha doesn't answer to their concern — he answers to their safety."

Samuel's mouth twitched. "That's not what you told me to say."

"Do it anyway."

When Samuel had disappeared into the trees, Victor stood alone by the moved boundary stone and stared north. The forest stretched for miles — dark, quiet, wrong. He could feel it pressing against the edge of his awareness like a hand on glass. If he squinted, he could almost see it: a faint shimmer, like heat rising from asphalt, except this wasn't heat. It was absence. A place where the world thinned to nothing.

He didn't tell Samuel about that part. The pack didn't need to know their Alpha could *feel* something wrong pressing against the edge of the world — not yet. They'd panic. They'd look for someone to blame. They'd demand action they weren't ready for.

Victor turned his back on the boundary and started the walk home.

---

The compound rose from the valley floor like it had grown there — stone and timber, wide windows catching the weak morning light. It sat in a secluded hollow surrounded by mountains on three sides, the valley opening only to the north where the boundary markers stood guard. The Blackwood pack had built it fifty years ago, when Cyrus — Victor's father, the Alpha before him, the man who had earned the position through blood and bond — decided the pack needed a permanent home.

Victor walked through the main gate without slowing. Two warriors at attention — Raine Blackwell and Marco Hayes — both straightened as he passed. Raine's hand went to the knife at her belt out of habit. Marco's mouth opened to make a joke, then closed when he saw Victor's face.

Evening was approaching. The sky had shifted from gray to a muted gold, and the air smelled of pine and damp earth and the woodsmoke from the central hearth. The compound's heart was the great hall — a long room with a vaulted ceiling, stone floor, and a fireplace large enough to stand in. That's where the pack gathered. That's where the food was. That's where the questions would start.

He had time.

Victor went to his quarters — a small room off the main hall, spartan but functional. Bed, desk, a shelf of dog-eared field manuals and pack histories. No personal items. No photos. No sign that a man lived there beyond the worn edges of the mattress and the knife he kept locked in the bottom drawer.

The room was on the ground floor. His father's quarters were on the second floor, larger, with a proper study and a window overlooking the valley. Victor had refused to move in the day Cyrus died. "Not yet," he'd told Josiah. "I haven't earned it." He was still telling himself that three years later. Every time he walked past the second-floor window, he felt the weight of the space beside him — not literal, but emotional. The empty bed. The empty desk. The empty Alpha. He wasn't Cyrus. He wasn't ready to take the room.

He stripped off his jacket, rolled his shoulders, and let out a breath he'd been holding since dawn. His left shoulder ached — an old scar from a pack dispute three years ago, when a rogue wolf had tried to challenge him. The rogue had lost. Victor had won. The scar didn't.

He washed his face, splashed cold water over his hands, and stared at his reflection in the small mirror above the sink. Dark hair. Gray eyes that shifted toward amber when his emotions ran high. Lines around his mouth from frowning more than smiling. He looked twenty-eight, but he felt forty.

The first shift had come on his sixteenth birthday, the way it always did. He remembered the pain — bones cracking and reforming, muscles tearing and knitting, the world shifting scale and color and scent until the human half of his mind was swallowed by something wild and ancient and *hungry*. He'd collapsed in the clearing behind the compound, shaking, terrified, and his father — Cyrus, the old Alpha — had knelt beside him and said:

*"You are a wolf now. That means you are part of something bigger than yourself. You will never be alone again."*

He hadn't been alone then. He wasn't alone now. But being part of something bigger didn't mean the weight was lighter. It meant someone else could share it. And his father was gone — killed by the dark three years ago, defending the northern boundary, and Victor had been too far away to help.

The Alpha position hadn't been handed to him — he'd earned it, bloodied knuckles and broken bones and sleepless nights included. But earning it and believing he deserved it were two different things. His father had built this pack from the ground up, held it together through war, through drought, through three rival packs trying to take their land. He had died a hero. Victor had survived his father. That wasn't the same thing. And every night, when the compound went quiet and the fire burned low, Victor lay awake and wondered if he'd made the right call that night. If he'd been faster, stronger, smarter — if he'd just been enough.

Victor opened the drawer, checked his knife, and closed it again.

He stood, straightened his jacket, and walked out of the quarters toward the great hall. He had time before the council. Time to think. Time to prepare.

But even as he walked, he felt it — the weight of the boundary on his shoulders, the missing hunter weighing in his gut, the pack's quiet worry humming beneath every conversation in the compound. He was the Alpha. He carried all of it.

And for now, that was enough.
