# Chapter 1 — The Triad Assessment

Silas Thorne had always been good at seeing things before other people did.

It started when he was small — before he understood what was happening. A dropped plate. A swinging door. A ball rolling toward the street. His body moved before his brain processed the threat. His hands were already reaching before his eyes registered danger. He didn't think about it. He just reacted.

His mother noticed first. She'd been hanging laundry in the courtyard of their apartment block when the line snapped — a simple metal clip failing under the wind's pressure. The laundry went flying. Her children were playing in the street below. Silas had been sitting on the steps, reading. He didn't look up. He just stood, grabbed his younger sister by the shoulder, and pulled her backward just as the laundry landed exactly where she'd been standing.

*How did you see that coming?* his mother had asked, heart hammering.

Silas had shrugged. *I just knew.*

He didn't just know. He *felt* it. Like a pressure in his chest, like the air changing before a storm breaks. He could feel things before they happened. It was never clear — just a sense, a whisper. And it was always right.

By the time he was twelve, he'd learned not to talk about it. People on Oakhaven didn't like things they couldn't explain. Oakhaven was a border world — distant, cold, agricultural. The soil was poor, the weather was harsh, and the Empire barely acknowledged its existence. The people who lived here worked hard and died young. They didn't have time for mysteries.

Silas had a sister — Mira, twelve years younger, with her mother's laugh and his stubborn streak. And a father — Tomas, who worked the fields from sunrise to sunset and came home with dirt under his fingernails and a back that ached by evening. His mother — Elena — ran a small greenhouse operation that supplied half the vegetables on Oakhaven. They were simple people. Good people. Not rich. Never rich. But proud.

The Triad Assessment changed everything.

Every five years, Vanguard Academy sent a selection team to each of the Empire's major worlds. They brought the Triad Assessment — three tests. Tactics, Physical Endurance, and Neural Link. The winner earned a place at Vanguard Academy on Station Helios. One of five hundred spots. Two percent acceptance rate. A ticket out of whatever world you were born on.

For Silas, it was more than a test. It was a way.

His parents had been saving for three years to send him to Oakhaven's assessment center. The travel costs alone had nearly broken them. But Elena had sold half her greenhouse stock. Tomas had taken extra shifts repairing the irrigation systems. They'd done it. They'd sent Silas to the assessment center on a Tuesday morning, packed him a lunch he wouldn't eat, and told him to do his best.

*You're going to do better than your best,* his mother had said, kissing him on the forehead. *You always have.*

He believed her.

---

The first test was strategy.

Silas Thorne sat in a windowless room on the border world of Oakhaven, his palms flat on a cold metal table, staring at a holographic display that showed an entire battlefield spreading out like a cracked mirror. Three hundred enemy vessels. Twelve of his own. Supply lines cut. Enemy fleet advancing from the eastern quadrant. Reinforcements impossible.

The AI instructor's voice came through the speakers: *You have four minutes to determine whether you live or die. Begin.*

Silas didn't hesitate. He looked at the numbers and did what he always did — he looked past them. The fleet wasn't the problem. The problem was the enemy's formation. They were advancing in a tight wedge, confident, predictable. They knew they had a 25-to-one advantage. They were going to walk through him.

Silas pulled six of his vessels forward. Not to fight. To die.

He arranged them in a line directly in the enemy's path and opened all comms channels on a frequency the enemy would automatically monitor. Then he broadcast three words: *Retreat now.*

The enemy fleet didn't slow down. They couldn't ignore a formation in their path. And the six sacrificial vessels — they weren't sacrificial. They were decoys. Each one carried a micro-payload of chaff and electronic countermeasures that would detonate when the enemy fleet passed through. Not to destroy. To blind.

Silas pulled the remaining six vessels into the darkness behind the enemy's wedge — where the sensors couldn't reach, where the formation couldn't protect, where the enemy's numbers meant nothing.

Four minutes. He gave the orders in a calm, flat voice. Six vessels moved. The enemy advanced. The chaff detonated. Sensors blinded. The six remaining vessels struck from behind, targeting the enemy command cluster. Three minutes and forty-two seconds.

The holographic display flickered. Green light. *Victory. Tactics score: A-rank.*

The wall behind the display slid open. A technician in a gray uniform stood there, clipboard in hand, looking at Silas like he'd just answered a question no one expected him to know.

*Tactics passed,* the technician said. *Proceed to Test Two.*

---

The second test was pain.

Silas stood in a centrifuge room on Oakhaven's eastern side, strapped into a harness that looked like it belonged on an old-fashioned amusement park ride. The room was cold. The air smelled like metal and sweat. Three other candidates stood in their own harnesses around him, looking equally nervous. None of them were from Oakhaven. That was the point. The Triad Assessment was designed to put border world candidates on equal footing with inner planet children who had been doing military drills since they could walk.

The instructor in the control booth above them pressed a button. The centrifuge started spinning.

Silas felt the G-force hit him like a fist to the chest. He gritted his teeth. The room tilted. The floor became a wall. He was pressed against the harness, his body screaming, his vision graying at the edges. The test was endurance — micro-gravity shifts, G-force endurance, combat survival drills. Four phases. Each one harder than the last.

Phase one: micro-gravity. He floated in a zero-G chamber for six minutes, navigating an obstacle course with his hands tied behind his back. He was strong for a border boy. He made it through.

Phase two: G-force endurance. The centrifuge spun faster. Six Gs. Eight Gs. Ten. His vision narrowed to a pinprick. His lungs felt like they were being crushed. He counted the seconds and pushed through.

Phase three: combat survival. He was dropped into a dark room and had to fight an opponent in complete darkness, using only sound and feel. He won by hearing the opponent shift their weight before striking. He didn't know who the opponent was. He didn't care. He won.

Phase four: the final test. The centrifuge spun to maximum — twelve Gs — and held. The instructor's voice came over the speakers: *You have thirty seconds. Hold.*

Silas held. His body screamed. His vision went black. He could feel his heart trying to beat its way out of his chest. Ten seconds. Twenty. Twenty-five. Twenty-nine. The centrifuge stopped.

He fell out of the harness and hit the floor hard. He lay there for a moment, staring at the ceiling, gasping, shaking. The technician was already there, clipboard in hand, looking at him with an expression he couldn't read.

*Physical Endurance: D-rank,* the technician said. *Border cadet. Barely passed.*

Silas pushed himself up. His arms were trembling. His body felt like it had been through a wringer. He nodded. Barely passed was still passed.

He walked out of the room and leaned against the wall in the hallway outside, closing his eyes and counting to ten before he moved again.

---

The third test was silence.

Silas sat in a dim room, the Neural Link interface headset resting on the table beside him. It looked like a pilot's helmet — streamlined, black, with a visor that reflected the light back at him in fractured pieces. The instructor sitting across from him was young, maybe mid-thirties, with a scar running down the left side of his jaw. He introduced himself as Instructor Voss. Tessa's father, Silas would learn later. Not that it mattered now.

*Sit in the simulator,* Instructor Voss said, nodding toward the corner. *Connect to the Neural Link. The test measures how well your brain synchronizes with the craft's systems. Normal range is fifty to eighty percent. Anything above eighty is considered exceptional. Anything above A-rank is theoretical.*

Silas sat in the simulator. It was a cockpit — a starfighter cockpit, from the looks of it. He could feel the contours of the seat, the texture of the controls, the hum of the simulator running. He picked up the Neural Link headset. It was heavier than he expected. He placed it over his head.

The world went dark.

Then light.

Then everything.

Silas felt the Neural Link connect to his brain like a hook sliding into his spine. The simulator responded instantly — every muscle in his body tensed, every nerve fired, every thought became an action. He didn't have to think about moving the craft. The craft moved when he thought about it. Not half a second later. Not a fraction of a second later. *Now.*

He saw the simulated battlefield in his mind — a star system with three planets, twelve enemy vessels, and a command center he needed to destroy. He didn't plan his approach. He didn't calculate vectors or trajectories. He just moved. And the craft moved with him — faster than any craft should be able to move, faster than any pilot should be able to react. The enemy vessels were destroyed before he even knew they were there. The command center blew apart. The simulation ended.

The headset came off.

Silas blinked. The room came back into focus. Instructor Voss was staring at the display on the wall behind him. His face was pale.

The display showed the Neural Link reading. A-rank. No. Above A. The system maxed out at A and couldn't process anything higher. It kept going past the limit, red warning text flashing across the screen:

*CRITICAL: LINK EXCEEDS MAXIMUM.*

*CRITICAL: LINK EXCEEDS MAXIMUM.*

*CRITICAL: LINK EXCEEDS MAXIMUM.*

Instructor Voss stood up so fast his chair rolled back and hit the wall. He walked to the display and stared at it like it had just told him something he didn't want to hear.

*How long?* he asked.

Silas didn't understand the question. *How long what?*

*How long were you connected?*

Silas thought about it. *Maybe thirty seconds.*

Instructor Voss's jaw tightened. He pressed a button on the wall and spoke into a comm channel: *This is Voss. We have a situation. Call the selection board.*

He turned back to Silas. *You're not like the others,* he said quietly. *I don't know what just happened. But it didn't happen before. Not to anyone.*

Silas looked at the display. *Above A-rank?*

*Beyond it.*

Silas felt something tight in his chest loosen, then tighten again. He'd been told his whole life that his reflexes were faster than everyone else's. That he could react before other people even saw what was happening. He'd thought it was just him. Just some quirk of his brain.

But it wasn't a quirk. It was something else. Something bigger. Something he couldn't explain.

---

The selection board meeting lasted an hour. Silas sat in a waiting room with the other three candidates from Oakhaven, none of whom had passed more than one test. He didn't know what they were saying. He didn't ask. He was thinking about the Neural Link reading. *Beyond A-rank.* What did that mean? He didn't know. He couldn't know. Not yet.

When the board called his name, he stood up and walked into the room. Five people sat around a long table, looking at him with expressions ranging from curiosity to concern. Instructor Voss sat at the head of the table.

*Silas Thorne,* the woman at the far end of the table said. She was older, silver-haired, with a voice that carried the weight of authority. *Border world of Oakhaven. Age: seventeen. Parents: agricultural workers on the outer rim.*

Silas nodded.

*Tactics score: A-rank. Physical Endurance: D-rank. Neural Link: unquantifiable.* She paused. *The system could not process your Neural Link reading. It exceeded the maximum measurable value. We have never seen this happen. In two hundred years, we have never seen this happen.*

Silas said nothing.

*The question,* the woman continued, *is whether you are a candidate or a hazard. Your Neural Link reading suggests something that goes beyond normal capability. We do not know what that means. We do not know if you can control it. We do not know if it is dangerous.*

She looked at the rest of the board. *The vote.*

One by one, the board members spoke.

*Passed three of three tests. Tactics is exceptional. Neural Link is unprecedented. Physical Endurance is the weakest link, but he passed. He passed.* Instructor Voss spoke first. *I say we accept him.*

*He's a border cadet,* another board member said. *He barely passed Physical. What happens when his Neural Link spikes in combat? What if he endangers his squad?*

*Then we train him to control it,* Instructor Voss said. *That's what we do.*

*You're biased,* the board member replied. *You saw what he did in the simulator. You think it's special.*

*It is special.*

The board deliberated for another ten minutes. Silas sat still, hands on his lap, face blank. He was used to reading people. He was used to calculating risk. But this room — this room was different. These people weren't evaluating a strategy. They were evaluating a person. A person they didn't understand.

Finally, the woman at the head of the table spoke. *The vote is unanimous. Silas Thorne is accepted to Vanguard Academy on Station Helios. Acceptance is contingent upon your completing Physical training to a minimum C-rank within your first year. Failure to meet this standard will result in immediate dismissal.*

Silas felt something shift in his chest. Acceptance. He'd passed. He'd actually passed.

*Your transport ticket to Station Helios will be processed within forty-eight hours,* the woman said. *You will be briefed on the Academy's curriculum, expectations, and consequences. You are one of five hundred cadets accepted this year. The acceptance rate is approximately two percent. You will not find this honor easy to earn. You will not find it easy to keep.*

Silas stood up. *Thank you.*

He walked out of the room, past the other candidates, out into the hallway, and into the cool evening air of Oakhaven.

The border world was quiet. The sky was dark, dotted with stars that looked too close, like you could reach up and touch them. Silas had grown up looking at these stars. He had grown up hearing people talk about them — about the Empire, about the inner planets, about the war that nobody on the border cared about.

He had never imagined he'd be going to the Academy.

Now he was.

One of five hundred.

And his Neural Link was something the system couldn't measure.

Silas walked back toward the transport terminal, shoulders squared, mind already working. He had a year to get his Physical Endurance from D to C. He had forty-eight hours to get his ticket and arrive on Station Helios. He had to figure out what his Neural Link meant. He had to figure out how to control it. He had to figure out why the board was afraid of him.

It was a lot of variables.

But that was okay. Silas Thorne was good at variables.

---

Forty-eight hours later, he was standing on a transport shuttle, looking out the viewport at Station Helios growing larger in the distance — a massive structure orbiting a gas giant, bristling with weapons, docking bays, and training platforms. It looked like a fortress. It looked like a city. It looked like the most intimidating thing Silas had ever seen.

He pressed his hand against the viewport and watched the station fill the sky.

Then the shuttle began its approach.

And his first year at Vanguard Academy began.
